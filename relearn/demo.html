AI / Thinking

Relearn how to think

Listen

12 min

My biggest lesson from two years of working intensively with AI has surprisingly little to do with AI. I have to relearn how to think.

Not how to prompt better. Not which model to use. Not how to build agents. How I think about what is possible in the first place.

I still catch myself doing this all the time. I have an idea or run into a problem and my immediate reaction is:

I don't know how to do that.

That sounds complicated.

That'll take forever.

We'll need someone for this.

And sometimes that's where the thought ends.

Then, an hour later or sometimes days later, I catch myself: Wait. Why did I just assume that? Why didn't I ask?

Our intuition is running on old data

For most of my career, this reflex was useful. Experience teaches you to recognize complexity before you walk into it. You don't just see a feature. You see everything behind it. Architecture. Dependencies. Edge cases. Testing. Debugging. Iterations. People. Time. Money.

Over decades, you develop a pretty good intuition for what things cost. An idea appears and your brain runs a feasibility check almost instantly: Can I do this? Do I know how? Do I have the skills? Who would I need? How long will it take? What will it cost? Is it worth it?

That mechanism isn't stupid. It's one of the benefits of experience. The problem is that the environment it was calibrated for has changed.

AI changed what's possible faster than we changed our intuition about what's possible.

We're still estimating the difficulty of new problems using the cost of solving them in the old world. And I notice this most in areas where I have a lot of experience. Experience still tells me what can go wrong. But I'm learning not to let it automatically tell me how difficult it will be to make it right.

I keep catching myself doing it

Recently, we were working on an internal platform for Founder.org. It connects information across the organization, orchestrates different agents for different tasks, automates workflows, and brings everything into one shared knowledge layer. Basically, a fully AI-powered operating system for the organization. Built, deployed, connected to a database, and used by the team every day.

At some point, I wanted Claude to interact directly with the platform. We'd need an MCP. My immediate reaction was basically: Fuck. That sounds complicated. Who builds that? How much work is this? Do we really need it?

And then I caught myself. I had just decided that the problem was difficult without even checking whether it was difficult now. So I asked. About an hour later, it worked.

Then another thought appeared. If Claude can read everything through the MCP, could I also tell it to add a note somewhere? Again, that tiny moment: Probably not. So I asked. Turns out, yes.

I had a similar experience integrating a payment terminal into another product. My old mental model immediately produced the entire nightmare. Documentation. APIs. Provider support. Configuration. Testing. Integration issues. Then I showed Claude the terminal. It identified what we were dealing with, walked me through the setup, prepared what it could, and within minutes a large part of it was working.

The surprising part wasn't that AI could do it. It was how wrong my initial estimate of the problem had been.

"I don't know how" has changed meaning

This is probably the biggest shift I'm trying to make in my own thinking. For most of my life, I don't know how was useful information. It meant I probably needed to learn something, find someone who knew how, spend money, invest significant time, or reconsider whether the idea was worth pursuing at all.

Today, I'm trying to treat that same thought differently. Not as a conclusion. As a trigger.

I don't know how.

Okay.

Ask.

That sounds almost stupidly simple, but I still fail to do it. And the interesting thing is that asking doesn't mean committing to anything. It costs almost nothing to explain what I want, get a possible path, understand where the actual complexity lies, and decide what to do next.

That first exploration used to have a cost itself. Research it. Find someone. Call them. Explain the problem. Get an estimate. Maybe hire them just to find out whether the idea makes sense. Now I can often get enough clarity to make a better decision in minutes.

I even catch myself asking people questions out of habit that I could have explored with AI first. Not because AI always has the better answer. It doesn't. But often I'm asking someone else before I've even taken a minute to understand the problem myself.

So one rule I'm trying to internalize is: Don't use your first "I can't" as a conclusion. Use it as a prompt.

The harder habits to catch

There's another version of this that I find even harder to notice. The things I don't think about at all.

I still catch myself doing repetitive things manually simply because that's how I've always done them. There's no "I can't do this" reflex to interrupt. No decision that something is too difficult. The task just feels normal. So I do it.

And increasingly, that itself feels like a signal. Why am I still doing this myself?

Old thinking doesn't only stop us from doing new things. It keeps us doing old things the old way.

Some of these things take five minutes. Some take thirty. None of them feel important enough to rethink. But repeated often enough, they become hours of work that probably shouldn't exist anymore.

The interesting part is that I don't need to know how to automate them. That would be the old question again. I only need to notice them long enough to ask whether they still need to be done this way.

Ask doesn't mean do

There's an obvious trap here. Once you realize how much you can suddenly attempt, it's easy to swing too far in the other direction. I do that too.

I'll quickly do this with AI. Three hours later, I'm somewhere deep in a rabbit hole wondering why the supposedly simple thing still doesn't work.

AI has created a strange estimation problem. The old instinct says: This will take three weeks. The new AI-euphoric instinct says: Claude will do it in 20 minutes. Both can be wrong.

So relearning how to think doesn't mean assuming everything is easy now. It means becoming less confident in your assumptions about what something should cost. In either direction.

If testing the next step is cheap, test instead of speculate. Then look again. Is this going somewhere? How much more attention will it take? What is the potential upside? Is it worth it?

That last question matters more to me now than "Can I do it?" Because increasingly, the answer to can I? is some version of: Maybe. Let's find out. The harder question is whether it deserves my time, attention and energy.

Sometimes the answer should still be no

Imagine I want to automate performance marketing for my company. My old reflex might be: I'm not a performance marketer. I'll need an agency. Maybe that agency costs €20,000 a month.

Today, I wouldn't make that decision without exploring the alternative first. I'd ask AI how I could approach it. What systems I'd need. What could be automated. Where the complexity is. What the cheapest viable version looks like. Then I'd start testing.

Maybe after a day I have something surprisingly capable. Great. Maybe after five days I'm still fighting it, it's consuming all my attention, everything around me is starting to suffer, and the result still isn't reliable. Also great. Now I know. Paying the agency may suddenly be the best decision I can make.

The point isn't to do everything yourself. The point is to stop delegating, abandoning or dismissing things based purely on an intuition formed in a different technological reality.

Sometimes the answer will still be no. But it should be an informed no.

A larger space of possible

I think this is the part I'm only beginning to understand. For a long time, the boundary of what I could realistically attempt was closely connected to what I knew how to do. Then it expanded to the people around me. Who knows how to do this? Then money. Who can I pay to do this?

AI introduces another question: What can I figure out? And that is a much larger space. Not knowing how to do something is no longer necessarily the boundary of your capability.

That doesn't mean AI makes everything possible. It doesn't. It doesn't mean expertise no longer matters. And it definitely doesn't mean every idea deserves to be pursued. It means something much simpler:

More ideas deserve to survive the first five minutes.

Because I'm starting to realize how many ideas don't actually die when they meet reality. They die before reality. We reject them because they sound expensive, difficult, unfamiliar or outside our capabilities. Often based on assumptions that were completely reasonable a few years ago. But reasonable then doesn't mean accurate now.

That's the part I'm trying to relearn. When my brain says: I can't do this. I don't know how. That's too complicated. That'll take forever. We'll need someone for this. I don't want to automatically believe it anymore.

I want to notice it. Ask. Find out what the problem actually looks like today. Try the cheapest next step. Then decide whether the potential upside is worth my attention.

Sometimes the answer will still be no. But at least it'll be today's no, not yesterday's.
